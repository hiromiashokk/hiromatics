Ganita Prakash · Grade 7 · Chapter 8 · Working with Fractions

Learn

A fraction is just a piece of a whole. Let's slice up a pizza 🍕 to see what the top and bottom numbers mean — then learn to multiply and divide fractions with real-life examples.

Hi! I'm Pepper.

Grab a slice — I'll show you how fractions work, one bite at a time.

Start with a whole — one complete pizza. A whole means the entire, unbroken thing: nothing missing, nothing extra.

Fun fact: The word fraction comes from the Latin fractus, meaning "broken". A fraction is literally a broken-up whole!
🍕🍕🍕🍕

Now cut it into equal parts. Every slice must be the exact same size. The number of equal slices is the bottom number (the denominator).

Cut the pizza into 4 equal slices → the bottom number is 4.

🍕🍕🍕😋

Take a part away — eat a slice! The slices you take are the top number (the numerator). Eat 3 of the 4 equal slices and you've eaten 3/4 of the pizza.

Joke: Why should you never argue with a fraction? Because it always knows how to break things down! 😄
🍫

Multiply means "a fraction of a fraction". To multiply, multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1×1 / 2×2 = 1/4.

Real life: a recipe needs 2/3 cup of sugar, but you only want half the recipe. So you use 1/2 × 2/3 = 2/6 = 1/3 cup. 🧁

Fun fact: When you multiply by a fraction less than 1, the answer gets smaller — half of a half is only a quarter!
🍕👫👭

Divide can mean sharing. Share 3/4 of a pizza equally among 3 friends: 3/4 ÷ 3 = 3/4 × 1/3 = 3/12 = 1/4 each. 🍕

The trick is Keep · Change · Flip: keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, and flip the second number upside down (3 becomes 1/3).

Joke: Why did the fraction go to the party alone? Because it couldn't find a common denominator! 😆
🥤🥤🥤

Divide can also ask "how many fit?" How many half-litre bottles fill a 3-litre jug? 3 ÷ 1/2 = 3 × 2/1 = 6 bottles. 🥤

Dividing by a fraction smaller than 1 makes the answer bigger — because lots of little pieces fit inside!

Fun fact: "Keep · Change · Flip" works because dividing by 1/2 is the same as asking how many halves there are — and that doubles your count.
